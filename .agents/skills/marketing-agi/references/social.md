# Social (LinkedIn / X)

The feed is an auction for attention where the reader has pre-committed to leaving. The first line is 80% of the outcome; everything else is retention mechanics.

Run every post through `slop-patterns.md` before delivery — feeds are the environment where AI-tells are punished hardest and fastest, because the audience sees hundreds of posts daily and pattern-matches instantly.

## What actually governs reach (both platforms)

- **Dwell time and early engagement** decide distribution. The hook's job is to stop the scroll; the structure's job is to hold the read; the ending's job is to provoke a reply.
- **Replies outweigh likes.** Write posts that are answerable — a specific claim someone can add to or push against beats a complete lecture.
- **External links suppress reach** on both platforms (widely observed, not officially documented). Link in the first comment, or post natively and gate the resource behind a comment — comment-gating works precisely because comments are the heaviest signal.
- Native formats beat pasted ones: documents/carousels and native video on LinkedIn, threads and native images on X.
- Consistency compounds; virality doesn't. One post a day for 90 days beats one viral hit, because the algorithm and the audience both reward presence.

## The hook

The truncation point is the whole ad: ~200 characters ("...see more") on LinkedIn, first line of a thread on X. Write 10 hooks per post, pick with the copy panel (`copy.md`).

Hook families that survive:
- **Specific number + tension** — "We spent €40k on ads in March. €31k of it taught us nothing."
- **Contrarian against the audience's default** — must be genuinely held; performative contrarianism gets clocked
- **Confession** — a real mistake with a real cost attached
- **Pattern-interrupt observation** — the thing everyone sees and nobody says
- **Direct callout** — "If you run paid social and CPMs rose this quarter, this is why."
- **Result-first** — the after, then the how

Hooks that die: rhetorical questions with obvious answers, "I'm excited to announce", inspirational abstractions, anything that could open a thousand other posts.

**The hook must break mid-tension at the fold.** If the truncation lands after a complete thought, the reader has no reason to expand.

## LinkedIn mechanics

- 1,300-2,000 characters is the durable sweet spot for text posts; shorter for pure-punch takes
- One idea per post. Two ideas = two posts. This is also how one insight becomes a week of content.
- Line breaks every 1-2 sentences — density reads as effort, and effort reads as skippable
- End with a question that has a real answer space, or a flat statement bold enough to argue with. Never "Thoughts?"
- First 60-90 minutes decide distribution: reply to every early comment, substantively
- 0-3 hashtags; they are organizational residue, not reach
- Tag people only when they'll plausibly respond — dead tags read as reach-begging
- Comment-gating ("comment X and I'll send it") works and is standard practice; deliver fast and actually deliver, because the DMs are the point

## X mechanics

- Standalone posts: one sharp claim beats a compressed essay
- Threads: the first tweet must work alone — most readers never expand. Each subsequent tweet earns the next; the moment one coasts, the thread dies.
- Numbered threads ("1/9") set a contract; only use when the payoff justifies the length
- Quote-posting a relevant conversation with a genuine addition outperforms cold posting for small accounts
- Reply-guy strategy is legitimate distribution for accounts under ~10k: substantive replies on large relevant accounts, consistently
- Media (native image, chart, screenshot) reliably lifts standalone posts

## Voice — the anti-slop layer beyond the checklist

The recognizable AI voice on social, beyond individual tells:
- Every post shaped as setup → 3 bullets → uplifting synthesis
- Relentless positivity with no cost, no loss, no specific failure
- Advice with no scar tissue — claims that could only come from reading, not doing

The fix is **specificity with a price tag**: name the number, the month, the mistake, what it cost. `brand-context.md` proof fields feed this. If the user has no specifics to give, say the post will read as generic and ask for one real detail — one is enough.

**Never fabricate the specifics.** An invented "we lost $30k" story is worse than a generic post; it is a public falsifiable lie under the author's name.

## Repurposing tree

One substantial insight becomes, in order of effort:
1. The core LinkedIn post (the argument)
2. An X thread (the argument, decomposed)
3. A contrarian standalone (the sharpest single claim)
4. A "how we did it" follow-up (the mechanism)
5. A results follow-up posted later (the receipts)
6. A carousel/document (the visual walkthrough)

Write them as separate pieces with separate hooks — never truncate-and-paste across platforms, because the hook economics differ.

## Metrics honesty

Impressions measure the algorithm's mood, not the content's value. Judge by replies, profile visits, DMs, and gated-resource requests. A 50k-impression post that produces nothing is decoration; a 3k-impression post that starts ten buyer conversations is distribution. Say this when the user optimizes for the wrong number.
