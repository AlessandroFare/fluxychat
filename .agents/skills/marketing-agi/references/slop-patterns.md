# Slop patterns

Constructions that make copy read as machine-generated. Check every one before delivery.

This is not stylistic fussiness. Readers now pattern-match AI writing quickly, and once they do, they discount the *claim*, not just the prose. A tell in the headline costs the argument.

---

## Structural tells

**1. "Not just X, but Y."**
The single most recognisable construction. Also: "It's not about X — it's about Y." Rewrite as a flat statement of Y.
> ✗ It's not just a CRM — it's a growth engine.
> ✓ It closes the loop between the ad click and the signed contract.

**2. Reflexive tricolon.** Three-item lists as the default rhythm. Fine once. Damning as a pattern.
> ✗ Faster, smarter, and more reliable.
> ✓ It runs in under two seconds.

**3. Metronomic sentence length.** Every sentence 12-18 words. Human writing varies violently. Deliberately break the rhythm — a three-word sentence, then a long one.

**4. The summarising em-dash clause.** One per piece is style. Three is a signature.

**5. Symmetrical paragraphs.** Every paragraph the same length. Vary it.

**6. Question-then-answer openings.** "What if there were a better way? There is."

**7. The reassuring close.** Ending on "and that's what makes all the difference" or any variant that restates without adding.

---

## Lexical tells

Delete on sight unless load-bearing:

| Category | Words |
|---|---|
| Empty intensifiers | truly, genuinely, incredibly, remarkably, undeniably, absolutely |
| Frictionless family | seamless, seamlessly, effortless, frictionless, streamlined |
| Transformation family | transform, revolutionise, unlock, elevate, empower, supercharge |
| Journey family | journey, landscape, realm, tapestry, ecosystem (unless literal) |
| Abstract plurals | solutions, offerings, capabilities, experiences, insights |
| Corporate hedges | leverage, utilise, facilitate, robust, holistic, bespoke |
| Time filler | in today's fast-paced world, in an increasingly digital age, now more than ever |
| Discourse glue | moreover, furthermore, additionally, that said, it's worth noting |
| False humility | it's important to note, we'd be remiss not to mention |
| Vague scale | countless, myriad, a plethora of, numerous |

**Delve, navigate, foster, underscore, harness, pivotal, crucial, comprehensive** — all heavily flagged. Use only when precisely correct and no synonym fits.

## Semantic tells

**8. Claims with no referent.** "Industry-leading", "best-in-class", "world-class", "cutting-edge". These say nothing and signal that nothing specific was available.

**9. Hedged claims that should be flat.**
> ✗ Can help you potentially reduce costs by up to 30%.
> ✓ Cuts fulfilment cost 31% at [customer].

Every hedge is a small confession. Stack four and the reader stops believing any of it.

**10. Benefits with no mechanism.** "Save time" is a wish. "Save time by auto-matching invoices to POs" is a claim.

**11. Audience described by demographic rather than state.** "For SMBs" tells the reader nothing about whether it is for them. "For teams still approving invoices in email" does.

**12. Perfectly balanced both-sidesing.** Marketing copy that carefully weighs pros and cons reads as generated. Take a position.

**13. Emoji as structure.** 🚀 ✨ 💡 opening bullet points. Fine in a Slack message, fatal in copy.

## The read-aloud test

Read it out loud. Every time you stumble, stop, or hear yourself sounding like a press release, mark that sentence. This catches rhythm problems that no checklist does, and it is faster than working through the list above.

Second test: **would a person say this to another person at a bar?** Not the register — the substance. If the substance would embarrass someone said out loud, it is not a claim, it is filler.

---

## What good sounds like

Specific. Uneven in rhythm. Willing to be blunt. Contains at least one fact that could be checked and disproved.

> Most invoice tools promise automation and then hand you a queue to approve manually. Ours matches the PO, checks the total, and only escalates the ones that don't reconcile. Last quarter that was 6% of them.

Three sentences. One number. One admission that it does not handle everything. No word from the table above.

---

## The exception

Some brands genuinely speak in a polished, formal, corporate register — enterprise security, regulated finance, medical. **Register is not slop.** The test is not formality, it is *emptiness*. A formal sentence that makes a specific, falsifiable claim is fine. A casual sentence that says nothing is not.

Check `brand-context.md` for tone before flattening a client's actual voice into the same clipped style everyone else uses. Over-correcting toward punchy is its own tell.
