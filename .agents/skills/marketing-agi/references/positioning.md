# Positioning, offer, pricing

The most upstream module. When copy keeps failing, ads keep fatiguing, and the audit keeps finding "generic messaging," the problem usually lives here. Fixing words downstream of a positioning problem is redecorating a house with no foundation — say so when you see it.

Read `brand-context.md`; this module is also what *produces* a good one.

## Positioning

Positioning is not a tagline. It is the set of decisions about what to compare yourself to, so the market can price and place you without effort.

### The five inputs (work in this order)

1. **Competitive alternatives** — what customers would actually do without you. Includes spreadsheets, interns, and doing nothing. "Do nothing" is the market leader in most categories.
2. **Unique attributes** — what you have that the alternatives don't. Features, but also model, data, distribution, speed, price structure.
3. **Value** — what those attributes *enable*, translated into outcomes a buyer would pay for. Attributes are facts; value is the reason to care.
4. **Best-fit segment** — who feels that value most acutely. Positioning to everyone who *could* buy dilutes to nothing; position to who *must*.
5. **Category frame** — the market context that makes the value obvious. This is the highest-leverage choice: the same product is "a cheaper X," "a Y for [segment]," or "a new category," and each frame sets different expectations, competitors, and price anchors.

### The output statement

```
For [best-fit segment]
who [the situation/pain that makes them must-buy],
[product] is a [category frame]
that [the one value claim],
unlike [the primary alternative],
because [the unique attribute that makes the claim credible].
```

Every clause must survive the test: **could the nearest competitor paste their name in?** If yes, the clause is decoration. Rework until at least the "unlike/because" pair is unpasteable.

### Diagnostics — when positioning is the real problem

- Copy tests keep producing flat results across strong variants → the argument, not the words
- Sales calls require long explanations of what the product *is* → category frame failure
- Price objections dominate → wrong alternative anchor (they're comparing you to something cheaper than your real comparison)
- Wins cluster in a segment nobody is targeting → the market found your position before you did; follow it

## Offer design

The offer is the deal, and the deal usually matters more than its description. Four axes:

- **Dream outcome** — concrete, named, the thing they'd tell a colleague about
- **Perceived likelihood** — proof, specificity, guarantees, track record. *Most weak offers fail here: the promise is fine, nobody believes it.*
- **Time to first result** — compress it; restructure onboarding before rewriting the promise
- **Effort and sacrifice** — what they must do and give up; every removed step is worth more than an added feature

Strengthening moves, cheapest first:
1. **Risk reversal** — guarantee, trial, no-card, pay-on-results. If the client refuses all of them, ask why; the answer is usually that they don't trust their own retention, which is the actual problem.
2. **Speed restructure** — a "first value in 10 minutes" path, even if the full value takes weeks
3. **Named proof** — one specific, verifiable result outperforms five anonymous testimonials
4. **Stack clarity** — enumerate what's included with the value of each; unenumerated value is un-priced value
5. **Real scarcity/urgency only** — capacity limits, cohort dates, genuine price changes. Fabricated urgency is a brand tax paid forever. If nothing is scarce, use cost-of-delay.

## Pricing strategy

Not price-setting math — the strategic layer a marketer owns:

### Structure before number
- **The metric** matters more than the amount: per-seat, per-usage, per-outcome, flat. The right metric scales with the value the customer receives; the wrong one punishes their success (per-seat pricing on a product whose value is company-wide caps expansion).
- **Three tiers** as default: an anchor (top), a target (middle, where design intent points), an entry (bottom, sized so its ceiling pushes growth to the middle). One tier forfeits the anchor effect; five creates a comparison-shopping project.
- **"Contact us" as the only price** is a lead filter, not a strategy — it self-selects for enterprise motion and kills self-serve. Fine if deliberate; usually it's avoidance.

### The pricing page (route execution through `copy.md`)
- Buyers scan for: what does it cost, what do I get, which one am I. Answer all three above the fold.
- Anchor high: rightmost/first-seen expensive tier makes the target look reasonable
- Name tiers by buyer identity ("Solo / Team / Company"), not by adjective ("Basic / Pro / Ultimate")
- The FAQ under the table is objection handling, not admin — put the top 3 real objections there
- Annual/monthly toggle defaults to annual with the discount stated as money saved, not percent

### Raising prices
Existing-customer grandfathering (permanent or timed) converts a churn event into a loyalty event. New-price-for-new-customers first, communicate the increase as investment (what shipped since), never apologize for it in the copy. A price increase with a weak product-progress story is the one case to delay.

```
## Cascade
[What this changes downstream: which pages, ads, emails now need rework — route to copy.md]

```

## Honesty

Positioning recommendations are hypotheses until the market votes; present the top frame *and* the strongest rejected alternative with the evidence for each. Never recommend a price point as if derived — without willingness-to-pay data it is a structured guess, and the deliverable should say which research (even five customer interviews) would convert the guess into a decision. Do not manufacture "unique" attributes; if nothing is genuinely unpasteable, the finding *is* that, and it goes to the founder, not into the copy.
